Abstract

In this reproduced analysis, we utilized the existing database of serum data from Hewitson et al. (2001) which proposed a panel proteins that were meant to distinguish boys with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and those that were typically developing (TD). The data consisted of 154 subjects and 1,317 proteins. We reproduced a simplified workflow of the published analysis, and made multiple modifications in the methodology to examine the sensitivity the accuracy classification as we made those changes. We noted that the log-transformation applied to the data centered the raw protein values to zero, which were heavily right-skewed. Subjects with their number of outliers were compared to each other by group, and found that there were a handful that were outliers from their group, but there was not a large enough number of subjects to skew one group or another. Modifications to the methodology which included training models with a training partition of the data, choosing a higher selection of predictive proteins, and using a fuzzy intersection rather than a hard intersection, yielded a lower AUROC score compared to the original simplified analysis. Combinations of methods were attempted to find a better accuracy classification score, and discovered that a combination of using training partitioned data for the selection procedure, and fuzzy intersection led to a similar panel of 5 proteins which had the same classification accuracy (0.8387097). Without the split data and with a hard intersection, a panel of 7 proteins were created with a very optimistic classification accuracy (0.9354839).

Dataset

The data came from Hewitson et al. (2021), who collected serum samples from 154 boys aged 18 months to 8 years: 76 diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and 78 typically developing (TD) boys. Protein levels in each sample were measured with SomaLogic’s SOMAScan 1.3K assay, which gave 1,317 proteins per subject. The data also includes each subject’s group (ASD or TD) and for the ASD group, an ADOS score measuring symptom severity. The published analysis used 1,125 of the proteins after excluding 192, but our analysis keeps all 1,317.

To preprocess the data, we log10-transformed each protein’s raw levels, which are heavily right-skewed, and then centered and scaled each protein to z-scores. In the original pipeline, any z-score beyond +/- 3 was trimmed to +/- 3 to limit the influence of extreme values. For Q2, we also made an untrimmed version of the data so we could look at those outlying values directly. Summary of published analysis

In order to identify which proteins’ serum levels indicated the presence of ASD, Hewitson et al. utilized various methods, including random forest, multiple testing involving t-tests, correlation-based methods. The 10 proteins with the best respective metrics were selected from these three methods to train a logistic regression model. Random Forest involved creating bootstrap samples for each tree, then picking a random subset of predictors at each split, and combining predictions across different trees. MeanDecreaseGini (mean decrease in Gini Index) was used to measure a protein’s importance to predict ASD versus TD. The RF model was trained 100 times using normalized data, then each protein’s importance value was averaged over the 100 runs. Then, the RF-based prediction model picked the 10 proteins with highest average importance values. Multiple testing involved using t-test to identify if there was a significant difference in serum levels between different groups for a protein. Then, the proteins whose t-test indicated a significant difference were formed together in a panel. 1,317 different t-tests are carried out, and in principle, it is a conceptually simple task. However, familywise error rate rises when multiple tests are conducted, leading to higher rates of a Type I error (false positive). Bonferroni correction is implemented to handle this issue, adjusting the threshold at which the p-value indicates significant difference. False discovery (false rejections) are also another major issue, typically handled by the Benajmini-Hochberg correction or Benjamini-Yekutieli correction. Similarly, the 10 proteins with the highest t-values were selected for the prediction model. Lastly, a correlation-based method calculated each protein’s correlation with the ADOS (Autism diagnostic Observation Scores) total scores. The 10 proteins with the highest absolute value of their correlation coefficient were selected.

Once the proteins were picked, 6 were identified to be in both RF and t-test’s best groups. These 6 were considered the ‘core’ proteins, which left 18 proteins remaining. The correlation method had no proteins overlap with RF and t-test. A logistic regression model was then trained on these 6 proteins using an 80/20 split for the training and testing datasets. Area under the curve (AUC) was utilized as an evaluation metric for how well the model performed on the test dataset. The process was repeated 1000 times to reduce the amount of bias. Then, each of the 18 proteins were added one-by-one to investigate whether the model’s classification accuracy can be improved further.

Lastly, to account for any confounding factors, Hewitson et al. investigated the effects of ethnicity, co-morbid conditions / clinical diagnoses, age, and medication on 12 of the proteins. Ethnicity was tested by splitting the data into two groups, white and non-white. Allergies were tested by a similar method, splitting the data into two groups, participants who had allergies and who didn’t. Then, t-test was run on the two groups for each of the 6 core proteins. To examine the effects of age, Spearman’s rank correlation was implemented for each protein versus age distribution. For the effects of psychiatric medications, AUC values for the full dataset was compared with AUC values for the dataset excluding participants who reported medication usage.

Findings

Impact of preprocessing and outliers

To look at outliers, we removed the trimming step from preprocessing and counted how many of each subject’s 1,317 protein values fell beyond |z| > 3. If the log-transformed levels were normally distributed, we’d expect about 3.6 such values per subject, but the median was 8.5, so the data has heavier tails than normal across the board. Some subjects stood far apart from the rest, though: using an IQR rule on the per-subject counts (cutoff was approx. 29), we flagged 13 outlier subjects, and six of them had between 114 and 157 outlying values each. For several of these subjects, nearly all of the outlying values were in the same direction (subject 9, for example, had 126 unusually high values and none unusually low), which looks more like the whole sample running high, possibly from sample handling or concentration, than a few individual proteins behaving strangely. Outlier subjects were somewhat more common in the TD group (9 of 78) than in the ASD group (4 of 76), but the difference wasn’t statistically significant (Fisher’s test, p approx. 0.25), and the number of outlying values per subject didn’t differ between groups either (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p approx. 0.82). Within the ASD group, outlier counts were also unrelated to the ADOS score (Spearman’s p approx. -0.04, p approx. 0.72). Overall, a handful of subjects are clear outliers, but they aren’t concentrated in one group enough to suggest they’re driving the differences between ASD and TD.

Methodological variations

To see how the model could be improved, we experimented with several adjustments and compared the results of several variations of the model to find what worked best. The first adjustment to the model we made was to partition the data into a training and test data set before any processing. In the original model, this step was made after the optimal set of proteins was selected, meaning that the test sample influenced this decision. Moving this step to the start and basing all calculations and decisions solely on the training data set ensures that this does not happen. While this resulted in a decrease in the model’s accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, and ROC AUC, doing this prevents the protein selection to be biased to include the data in the test data sets and ensures the resulting metric are not overly optimistic.

We also adjusted the number of proteins chosen for analysis. The proteins were selected through two separate methods in the original model, and later combined using the intersection of both selections. For our new model, we tested different combinations of the number of proteins being selected from each model, like 5 proteins chosen by the t-tests and 15 proteins chosen by the random forest for example. The minimum number of proteins selected by each method was 5 and the maximum number selected was 40. The resulting amount of proteins, after taking either the intersection or union from the two selection methods, each model used ranges from 3 to 60. On top of that, each combination of proteins was tested with both with a hard intersection, like the original model where only proteins from both selections are used, and a fuzzy intersection, which uses all the proteins selected. Improved classifier

To find an improved classifier with a simpler panel for comparable or improved accuracy, we experimented with split data, fuzzy intersection, and selection of 10 predictive proteins. Our analysis is separated into two parts, trimmed and untrimmed data. With trimmed data, our classifier could only achieve a panel of 5 proteins with the same accuracy score as the original analysis (0.8387097). The 5 main proteins within the panel were DERM, RELT, MRC2, IgD, and Cadherin-5. Without split data and with a hard intersection, the next best accuracy score achieved was 0.9354839 with a panel of 7 proteins, which is significantly higher than the original. The 7 main proteins were again DERM, RELT, MRC2, IgD, PTN, Cadherin-5, and Notch 1. However, as established in examining the methodological variations, models without the split data provided highly optimistic accuracy scores which should warrant suspicion of the improvement. Therefore, the best panel achieved from splitting data and using a fuzzy intersection. We could not simplify further from 5 proteins and achieve a better accuracy score than the original model.

Next, we worked with untrimmed data, which avoided modifying protein’s levels whose z-score exceeded +/- 3. With untrimmed data and splitting, the results are nearly identical to those of the trimmed data. The conclusion is the same, where the classifier achieves a similar accuracy to the original analysis with a panel of 5 proteins. The 5 proteins are DERM, IgD, TSP4, PTN, and FSTL1. Unlike the trimmed data, the best accuracy achieved without splitting the data is 0.871 using hard intersection and a panel of 6 proteins, DERM, RELT, MRC2, IgD, PTN, and Cadherin-5. The accuracy does not significantly improve compared to the original analysis’. This suggests that the trimmed data plays a bigger role in improving the accuracy of the classifier on the full dataset. But as stated earlier, this slightly biases the classifier, making it an inaccurate representation of how well it generalizes to unseen data. Therefore, trimming versus untrimming the data does not significantly impact the classifier’s ability to provide accurate predictions when splitting the data.
